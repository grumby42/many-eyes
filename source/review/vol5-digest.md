## Impression: Paris, 1874 (1872–1876) [id impressionists-1874]

They had no jury and no prizes. Each member paid a subscription, the hanging was settled by lot within groups of similar size, and the society took a commission on sales. Four months later, on 15 April 1874, they opened a month-long show in the empty rooms of the photographer Nadar's former studio at 35 boulevard des Capucines. It ran two weeks ahead of the official Salon, which opened on 1 May at the Palais de l'Industrie. About thirty artists showed some 165 catalogued works. ## The Bauhaus: Weimar, Dessau, Berlin, 1919–1933 (1919–1933) [id bauhaus]
Intro: In April 1919, five months after the armistice and in the city where a national assembly was drafting Germany's first democratic constitution, the architect Walter Gropius merged two Weimar art schools and called the result the Bauhaus. His manifesto promised to put painters, sculptors and craftsmen back into one workshop and to build a new world together. In fourteen years the school moved twice, had three directors, and outlived three governments that wanted it closed, until its own faculty dissolved it in Berlin in July 1933 rather than accept the conditions of the new regime. This chapter sets the school's people side by side. Oskar Schlemmer's diary and letters are the frankest running commentary on its quarrels. ## Harlem, 1921–1931 (1921–1931) [id harlem]
Intro: In May 1921 a Black musical opened on West 63rd Street and ran for more than a year; in June The Crisis printed a short poem about rivers by a twenty-year-old named Langston Hughes. Over the next ten years Harlem became the place where Black American writers, artists and musicians made a claim on the nation's culture, and where white New York came to watch, buy and judge. This chapter follows that decade from the inside of its rooms: a club dining room on West 12th Street, a Fifth Avenue restaurant, a townhouse salon on 136th Street, a magazine office downtown, a patron's Park Avenue apartment. Most of the record is letters and dated magazine issues rather than diaries, and the form tags say so. The one true diarist, Alice Dunbar-Nelson, watched from Wilmington, Delaware.
