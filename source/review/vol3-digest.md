## Embassies to Samarkand and Beijing (1402–1422) [id embassies-1403]
Intro: In the summer of 1402 Timur broke the Ottoman army at Ankara and captured its sultan. Kings in the far west took notice: Enrique III of Castile sent envoys east, and in May 1403 a second embassy under his chamberlain Ruy González de Clavijo sailed from Cádiz for Samarkand. At the other end of Asia, the usurping Yongle Emperor had just taken the Ming throne, and Timur, who had imprisoned Ming envoys for years, was preparing to invade China. This chapter follows the same two empires seen from opposite directions. Clavijo reached Samarkand in September 1404, in the last autumn of Timur's life, and watched Ming ambassadors humiliated at a reception before Timur marched east in midwinter and died at Otrar in February 1405. ## The Narrow Road (1689 (with 1186 and 1690–92)) [id narrow-road]
Intro: In the spring of 1689 Matsuo Bashō, the most admired haikai master in Edo, sold his hut by the Sumida River and walked north with his disciple Kawai Sora. For about five months they followed the Pacific side up to Hiraizumi, crossed the mountains to the Sea of Japan, came back down the long coast of Echigo and the Hokuriku, and ended at Ōgaki in Mino. Out of it Bashō made Oku no Hosomichi, 'the narrow road to the interior', the most famous travel book in Japanese. This chapter is built on a rare gift: two records of the same journey by the two men who made it. Bashō's is not a diary. He rewrote it for years, had it fair-copied, and it was printed only in 1702, after his death. Sora's notebook, published only in 1943, is the opposite: dates, hours, distances, lodgings, weather, names — and a separate record of the verse sessions as they actually happened. ## The Transit and the Shore (1768–1771) [id endeavour]
Intro: In August 1768 a converted Whitby collier, HM Bark Endeavour, left Plymouth with about ninety people aboard. Its public errand was astronomy: on 3 June 1769 Venus would cross the face of the sun, and timings from Tahiti, set against those from the far north, might fix the distance from the earth to the sun. Its sealed second order was to look for the great southern continent. This chapter puts the travellers' records beside those of the people they met. ## Humboldt's America (1799–1804) [id humboldt]
Intro: On 5 June 1799 a Spanish mail ship left A Coruña with a Prussian mining official turned naturalist, Alexander von Humboldt, and a French botanist, Aimé Bonpland, aboard. They carried an unusual royal licence from Carlos IV that let two foreigners roam Spain's American empire with barometers, chronometers and plant presses. For five years they crossed what are now Venezuela, Cuba, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru and Mexico, and ended with a few crowded weeks in Philadelphia and Washington as Thomas Jefferson's guests. Humboldt's books later reshaped how Europeans pictured the Americas and how scientists thought about nature as one connected system. This chapter tries to look at that journey from more than one side. ## Ocian in View: The Corps of Discovery (1803–1806) [id corps-of-discovery]
Intro: On 14 May 1804 a keelboat and two pirogues pushed off from Camp Dubois, opposite the mouth of the Missouri, and began rowing against the current. What makes this expedition unusual for a book about many eyes is that so many of its members wrote. Jefferson had ordered the captains to keep journals and urged others to do the same, and at least seven men did: the two captains, Meriwether Lewis and William Clark, and the enlisted journalists Sergeant John Ordway, Sergeant Charles Floyd, Patrick Gass (a private who became sergeant after Floyd died), Private Joseph Whitehouse and Private Robert Frazer, whose journal is lost. Gary Moulton's edition, now online from the University of Nebraska, puts every keeper's entry for a given day on one page. So when the boats left, when Floyd died, or when Clark thought he saw the ocean, we can read four or five accounts of the same afternoon side by side.

Intro: In June 1857 two officers of the East India Company's armies, Richard Burton and John Hanning Speke, walked inland from the coast opposite Zanzibar to test what Arab and Swahili traders already knew: that great lakes lay beyond Unyamwezi. They spent months at the courts of Rumanika of Karagwe, Muteesa I of Buganda and Kamurasi of Bunyoro, and on 28 July 1862 Speke watched the lake pour out over the rocks he named Ripon Falls. ## Ujiji, 1871: Two Diaries, One Voice (1866–1874) [id livingstone-1871]

## Fram: The Ship That Drifted and the Men Who Walked (1893–1896) [id fram]
Intro: In June 1893 Fridtjof Nansen sailed from Christiania in a ship built to be trapped. Fram's rounded hull was designed so that the pack ice, instead of crushing her, would squeeze her upwards; Nansen's theory was that a current carried the ice from Siberia across the polar basin, and a ship frozen in north of the New Siberian Islands would drift with it towards the Pole. In late September 1893 she was frozen in. For almost three years she drifted, slowly and often backwards, with thirteen men aboard. When it became clear the drift would pass too far south of the Pole, Nansen left the ship on 14 March 1895 with one companion, the stoker Hjalmar Johansen, three sledges, 28 dogs and two kayaks. On 8 April they turned back at 86°14′, the farthest north anyone had reached. ## Everest, 1953 (1924; 1952–1955 (core: March–June 1953)) [id everest-1953]
Intro: In spring 1953 a British expedition led by John Hunt set out to put two men on the top of Everest (Chomolungma to Tibetans, Sagarmatha in Nepal) by the south route, which had only opened to outsiders when Nepal let in foreign climbers after 1949. The year before, a Swiss team had shown the way: on 28 May 1952 Raymond Lambert and Tenzing Norgay had climbed to about 8,595 m on the south-east ridge before failing oxygen and exhaustion stopped them. Behind both lay an older, separate story, told here only as a prologue: George Mallory and Andrew Irvine, last seen by Noel Odell on the north-east ridge on 8 June 1924, and never seen alive again. This chapter follows the climb through the people who wrote it down.
