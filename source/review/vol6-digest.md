## The House of Worship: Fatehpur Sikri, 1575–1582 (1574–1583) [id fatehpur-sikri]
Intro: In 1575 the Mughal emperor Akbar, then in his early thirties and master of most of northern India, had a hall built beside the mosque of his new red-sandstone city at Fatehpur Sikri. He called it the Ibadat Khana, the House of Worship. On Thursday nights, the eve of the Muslim Friday, he sat there with Sunni scholars, Sufi shaikhs, nobles and sayyids and put questions to them. Within four years the circle had widened to Shia scholars, Brahmans, Jain monks, Zoroastrian priests from Gujarat and, in February 1580, three Jesuits from Goa. Between 1579 and 1582 Akbar read the Friday sermon himself, received a signed decree from his ulama recognising his authority to choose between conflicting legal opinions, and finally drew a small circle of disciples into a devotional order of his own. ## The Long Room: Philadelphia, Summer 1787 (1787) [id philadelphia-1787]
Intro: For sixteen weeks in the summer of 1787, about fifty-five men met behind closed doors in the Assembly Room of the Pennsylvania State House. They had been sent to amend the Articles of Confederation and wrote a new constitution instead. Their first rules forbade printing or repeating anything said in the room, and the rule held: the public saw nothing until the finished text appeared in the Pennsylvania Packet on 19 September. So the room is known almost entirely through private papers, and the form tags say so. Only the secretary, William Jackson, kept an official Journal, and it records motions and state votes, never a speech. Everything else comes from delegates' notes (Madison, Yates, King, McHenry, Pierce, Paterson, Hamilton), letters home (Mason, Gerry, Washington, Hamilton), Washington's spare diary, Franklin's written speeches, and a visitor's journal (Cutler). The Emperor of Russia and the King of Prussia rode into the city on 25 September; behind them came ministers, princes of defunct states, petitioners for cities, churches, Jewish communities and booksellers, and a crowd of hostesses, artists and spies. The Congress as a plenary assembly never met. The decisions were made in small rooms by five or so ministers, and announced in protocols drafted by a secretary. This chapter lays those rooms beside the paper that came out of them. On 16 July they drafted a Declaration of Sentiments at the M'Clintock family's parlour table, modelling it on the Declaration of Independence.
