## Lighter Than Air: France and England, 1783–1785 (1782–1785) [id balloons-1783]
Intro: In the eighteen months between June 1783 and January 1785 people went from watching a paper globe rise over a market town in the Vivarais to crossing the sea by air. The idea, the first trials and a kind of mastery all fall inside two years, and they happened in public, before kings, crowds of tens of thousands, and some of the most careful letter-writers of the age. Two methods raced each other. The Montgolfier brothers, papermakers from Annonay, filled their envelopes with smoke and heated air; the physicist Jacques Charles and the Robert brothers used the 'inflammable air' (hydrogen) that chemists had only lately learned to make. Within weeks of each other, Paris saw an unmanned hydrogen globe vanish into cloud, a sheep, a duck and a cockerel go up before Louis XVI, men lifted on a rope in a wallpaper maker's garden, and finally two men carried freely across the city. ## Rainhill and the Opening Day, 1829–1830 (1829–1830) [id rainhill-1830]
Intro: In the spring of 1829 the Liverpool & Manchester Railway had a line almost built and no settled way to move trains along it. Two respected engineers, James Walker and John Rastrick, had toured the colliery railways of the North-East and reported that ropes wound by fixed engines in engine-houses along the route would be cheaper than locomotives. The directors chose not to decide on paper. They offered £500 for a locomotive that could meet set conditions, and in October 1829 five machines came to a level stretch at Rainhill to try for it. The trials are recorded unusually well, because they were a test with rules. Rastrick, one of three judges, kept a notebook of weights and timings; the Mechanics' Magazine and the Liverpool papers reported every day; the Stephensons and their rival Timothy Hackworth left letters. ## Joining the Oceans and the Continent, 1869 (1854–1870) [id oceans-1869]
Intro: In 1869 two pieces of the world's transport map were joined six months apart. On 10 May at Promontory Summit, Utah, the Central Pacific building east from Sacramento met the Union Pacific building west from Omaha, and a telegraph operator sent one word across the country. On 17 November the Empress Eugénie's yacht L'Aigle led some forty ships into the Suez Canal, and three days later the procession reached the Red Sea. A continent had been crossed by rail and two oceans by a ditch through the desert. The honest record of both is mostly not diaries. It is company letters and payrolls, chief engineers' reports, dated photographs, speeches as the newspapers printed them, a crown prince's privately printed travel diary, a painter's notebook, and a poet's verse letter written a year later. ## Kitty Hawk, 1896–1903 (1896–1905) [id kitty-hawk]
Intro: In the summer of 1896 two men stood at opposite ends of the idea of human flight. On 6 May Samuel Pierpont Langley, Secretary of the Smithsonian Institution, watched a steam-driven model with no one aboard circle above the Potomac for more than half a mile while Alexander Graham Bell photographed it. Three months later Otto Lilienthal, who had made some two thousand glides from hills near Berlin, stalled in a gust, fell and died the next day. One line of work trusted power and stability; the other trusted the pilot's skill. In Dayton, Ohio, two bicycle makers read about Lilienthal's death, and within three years Wilbur Wright was writing to the Smithsonian for whatever it had printed on flight. ## Across the Channel and the Atlantic, 1909–1927 (1908–1927) [id channel-atlantic]
Intro: In July 1909 the English Channel, about twenty-two miles of grey water between Calais and Dover, was the obvious test for the aeroplane. The Daily Mail had offered £1,000 for the first crossing. Hubert Latham went first and came down in the sea. Six days later Louis Blériot, limping from a burned foot, crossed in roughly thirty-seven minutes in a small monoplane with no compass, and landed hard in a meadow under Dover Castle while a French reporter waved a tricolour to guide him in. Within three years the Channel was a route rather than a feat. When Harriet Quimby flew it in April 1912, the first woman to do so, she flew by compass through fog, and the news was buried under the loss of the Titanic. The next obstacle was the Atlantic.
