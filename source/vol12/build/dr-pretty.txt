 "intro": [
  "On 28 May 1830 Andrew Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act, which let the President offer lands west of the Mississippi in exchange for the homelands of nations in the East. Mississippi had already extended its state laws over the Chahta (Choctaw) Nation early that year. In mid-September the Secretary of War, John H. Eaton, and General John Coffee rode in carriages to a council ground on Chukfi Ahihla Bok, Dancing Rabbit Creek, in what is now Noxubee County. Several thousand Chahta people camped there, by district, under their three chiefs: Greenwood LeFlore, Mushulatubbee and Nitakechi.",
  "The council lasted about two weeks. The Chahta council voted down removal; many families went home believing the talks were over; the commissioners then negotiated with the leaders who remained. On 27 September a treaty was signed that ceded all Chahta land east of the Mississippi and set removal for the falls of 1831, 1832 and 1833. Its fourteenth article promised land in Mississippi to any head of a family who wished to stay and registered within six months. Supplementary articles of 28 September added reservations for named individuals.",
  "The honest record here is mostly letters, official papers and later testimony, not diaries. The commissioners kept a journal, now held in the National Archives (Record Group 75) and published with images by the Papers of Andrew Jackson; it could not be read directly for this chapter, so the council days are reconstructed from the treaty text, the commissioners' own letters as printed in 1830, newspaper correspondents, missionary letters, and the Chahta testimony collected by Henry S. Halbert and published in 1902, which the Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma itself uses to tell the story. Chahta speeches survive only as interpreters' English (the United States interpreters were John Pitchlynn and M. Mackey) or as accounts told decades later.",
  "After the signing the record moves to the river. George S. Gaines took an exploring party west in October 1830 and superintended the first removal in 1831–32. Peter Pitchlynn, who opposed the treaty, led a party of about five hundred and kept a diary as he scouted the new country in January 1832. George W. Harkins wrote his farewell to the American people on a steamboat. Alexis de Tocqueville, waiting at Memphis in December 1831, watched a band of Chahta people cross the frozen-edged river and wrote to his mother about it on Christmas Day.",
  "The removals of 1831–33 moved some thirteen thousand people; the first winter was the hardest, and deaths along the route and in the first years in the West ran into the thousands by historians' estimates. Several thousand Chahta stayed in Mississippi, many of them failed by the fourteenth article's registration, whose shortfalls Congress was still investigating in 1837–38. Their descendants are today's Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians; the removed nation rebuilt in what is now Oklahoma."
 ],
