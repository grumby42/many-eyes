intro [
"Between 1960 and 1970 the idea of chopping messages into small blocks and letting computers pass them from switch to switch went from a Cold War survivability study to a working network. It did not have a single inventor. Paul Baran at RAND in Santa Monica and Donald Davies at the National Physical Laboratory outside London reached the same design separately, for different reasons, and neither built it first.",
"The American network came out of ARPA's Information Processing Techniques Office, where Bob Taylor wanted his agency's computers linked and Larry Roberts drew up the plan. In October 1967, at a symposium in Gatlinburg, Tennessee, a British engineer, Roger Scantlebury, explained packets to Roberts and pointed him to Baran's unread reports. A year later Bolt Beranek and Newman in Cambridge won the contract to build the switches, the Interface Message Processors, and a senator's congratulatory telegram got the name wrong.",
"The record here is mostly not diaries. It is dated reports and memoranda (RAND's, NPL's, BBN's quarterly reports to ARPA), the Request for Comments series that graduate students began in April 1969, one handwritten log book at UCLA, and a great deal of later recollection. Where a date or detail rests only on recollection, the entry says so.",
"The centre of the chapter is one evening: Wednesday 29 October 1969, about 22:30 Pacific Standard Time. Charley Kline at UCLA tried to log in to Bill Duvall's computer at SRI, 350 miles north, the two of them talking on the telephone as each letter went. Two letters arrived before the system crashed. The log records it in one line, and the later accounts disagree on details.",
"Who invented packet switching is still argued. Baran and Davies are credited by most historians with independent invention; Leonard Kleinrock, whose 1961–64 queueing work analysed message-switched networks, has since the 1990s claimed to have laid its theoretical foundation, and Davies, Taylor and others have rejected that. The chapter sets out the positions and the evidence and leaves the verdict to the reader. A short coda follows the network to its first email in 1971, its public demonstration in Washington in October 1972, and the French CYCLADES network whose ideas fed the internet's protocols."
