intro ["In September 1893, beside the white plaster palaces of the World's Columbian Exposition, Chicago staged what its organisers called the first Parliament of the World's Religions. For seventeen days, in the new Art Institute on Michigan Avenue, clergy of every Christian denomination shared a platform with a Hindu monk, a Jain lawyer, Brahmo reformers, Buddhists from Ceylon and Japan, an envoy of the Chinese emperor and an American convert to Islam. Christians gave about 152 of the 194 papers; the handful of Asian speakers took most of the attention.", "The record is mostly speeches and letters rather than diaries. The speeches survive as printed by John Henry Barrows, the Presbyterian pastor who chaired the Parliament and edited its two-volume history, sometimes in condensed form; several of the visitors never spoke in English at all, and their words were read for them by Barrows or others. The fullest private voice is Vivekananda's, whose letters to his disciples in Madras and to his new American friends track his journey from penniless stranger to paid celebrity. Dharmapala kept diaries, published only in extracts; Pung Kwang Yu's paper went home as an official report to the throne.", "The hosts looked back through their own lenses. Barrows welcomed the 'wise men of the East' while holding that Christianity was the fulfilment of all faiths; the Archbishop of Canterbury refused to come at all. Newspapers in Chicago and in the small cities of the Midwest described the visitors' robes, turbans, voices and complexions before their arguments, swinging between admiration and racial condescension. Those descriptions are reported here as written.", "The visitors were not neutral either. Hirai Kinzō told the hall that Christian nations had imposed unequal treaties on Japan and barred Japanese children from American schools; Pung, Dharmapala and Vivekananda all charged missionaries with ignorance or arrogance. Vivekananda's letters praise American women and wealth and pour scorn on Indian caste, turning the comparison against his own country as much as his hosts'.", "A coda follows Vivekananda on the lecture circuit from Madison to Detroit to Baltimore, to a summer of teaching on the St Lawrence and to London and Oxford, where the gaze reverses again: the Indian monk calling on the European scholar who had interpreted India to the West."]
calendar_note "All dates are Gregorian. Japan had adopted the Gregorian calendar in 1873, so Japanese records of the delegation's departure in Meiji 26 (1893) fall on the same days. Pung Kwang Yu's report Shuo jiao was submitted and printed in Guangxu 22, which ran from February 1896 to January 1897; it is filed here under 1896. Several Parliament days are not confirmed for individual speakers and are marked as ranges with confidence 'approx'."
