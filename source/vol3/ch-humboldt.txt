.intro[0] = On 5 June 1799 a Spanish mail ship left A Coruña with a Prussian mining official turned naturalist, Alexander von Humboldt, and a French botanist, Aimé Bonpland, aboard. They carried an unusual royal licence from Carlos IV that let two foreigners roam Spain's American empire with barometers, chronometers and plant presses. For five years they crossed what are now Venezuela, Cuba, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru and Mexico, and ended with a few crowded weeks in Philadelphia and Washington as Thomas Jefferson's guests. Humboldt's books later reshaped how Europeans pictured the Americas and how scientists thought about nature as one connected system.
.intro[1] = This chapter tries to look at that journey from more than one side. Humboldt's own record is huge: the bound field diaries (the Amerikanische Reisetagebücher, bought for the Berlin State Library in 2013 and now digitised) and dozens of dated letters, above all to his brother Wilhelm. Next to them sit the voices of the people he met. Francisco José de Caldas, a self-taught creole astronomer from Popayán, wrote dated letters full of delight and then hurt. José Celestino Mutis ran the royal botanical expedition in Bogotá. Viceroy Pedro Mendinueta sent a confidential warning to Madrid, and a Spanish naval commander filled his log with suspicion. In the United States, Jefferson wrote letters, Charles Willson Peale kept a diary and Albert Gallatin wrote to his wife.
.intro[2] = The heart of the chapter is a single night. In the small hours of 12 November 1799 the Leonid meteors came down as a storm. Humboldt and Bonpland watched from Cumaná. The American surveyor Andrew Ellicott watched from a ship in the Florida Keys, and Moravian missionaries in Labrador recorded the terror of an Inuit man named Kapik. Months later, deep in the Orinoco missions, Humboldt found that Spanish friars had written the same night into their parish books. One sky, written down in Spanish, English, German and French by strangers who never met.
.intro[3] = Honesty about form matters here. Most of the hosts' voices are letters and official papers, not diaries, and they are tagged that way. The Indigenous peoples of the Orinoco and the Andes left no written record of these meetings that we could verify. That includes the paddlers, the porters who stopped at the snow line on Chimborazo, and José de la Cruz of Cumaná, who carried the instruments for five years. They appear here only through Humboldt's pages and one painting, and the entries say so. Bonpland's own botanical journal is a run of numbered plant descriptions that are mostly undated, so much of what we know of him also comes through Humboldt.
.intro[4] = Read side by side, the sources show something Humboldt's later books smooth over. He depended on creole scientists who wanted to join him, on colonial officials who were watching him, and on Indigenous guides who knew the rivers and passes far better than he did. When he reached Washington, the first thing a President on the edge of a border quarrel with Spain wanted from him was numbers about New Spain.
.calendar_note = All dates are Gregorian (New Style). Spain, its colonies and the United States all used the Gregorian calendar by 1799. One printed source used here, Delamétherie's 1804 summary of the journey in the Journal de Physique, is dated in the French Republican calendar ('Thermidor an XII', about late July to mid-August 1804). Many of Humboldt's diary pages were written up or recopied days or weeks after the events, so a diary 'date' can mean the day of the event rather than the day of writing. Where only a month or season is known, precision is set to 'month' or 'range'.
