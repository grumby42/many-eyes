.intro[0] = On 14 May 1804 a keelboat and two pirogues pushed off from Camp Dubois, opposite the mouth of the Missouri, and began rowing against the current. What makes this expedition unusual for a book about many eyes is that so many of its members wrote. Jefferson had ordered the captains to keep journals and urged others to do the same, and at least seven men did: the two captains, Meriwether Lewis and William Clark, and the enlisted journalists Sergeant John Ordway, Sergeant Charles Floyd, Patrick Gass (a private who became sergeant after Floyd died), Private Joseph Whitehouse and Private Robert Frazer, whose journal is lost. Gary Moulton's edition, now online from the University of Nebraska, puts every keeper's entry for a given day on one page. So when the boats left, when Floyd died, or when Clark thought he saw the ocean, we can read four or five accounts of the same afternoon side by side.
.intro[1] = Those enlisted journals are not echoes of the captains. Ordway counts the men and the guns, Whitehouse writes down what the cook and the woodcutters did, and Gass's 1807 book (the first account of the expedition in print, heavily smoothed by his Pittsburgh editor David McKeehan) keeps the rhythm of a carpenter's working day. Where the captains write as diplomats and naturalists, the sergeants record the cost in labour, sickness and weather.
.intro[2] = The heart of this chapter is the encounter, and here the record is lopsided. The nations the Corps met (the Otoe, the Teton Lakota, the Arikara, the Mandan and Hidatsa, the Lemhi Shoshone, the Nimiipuu or Nez Perce, the Clatsop and Chinook) kept their own histories, but mostly not on paper. One dated Indigenous record does survive for these years: the Dakota winter counts, in which a keeper painted one image for each year. Lone Dog's count, kept among the Yanktonai, marks 1804–05 with a calumet dance before war and 1805–06 with eight men killed by the Crow. It does not mention the strangers on the river. That silence is itself evidence of how small the expedition looked from the plains. The Nimiipuu remember the arrival at Weippe Prairie through an oral tradition about a woman called Wetxuuwíis. It was recorded from tribal elders in 2001–02 and is flagged here as oral tradition. Sacagawea and the Mandan leader Sheheke (Big White) left no writing of their own. They appear here only through other people's pages, and their entries say so.
.intro[3] = Two rival empires were also watching. At the Mandan villages that winter, François-Antoine Larocque of the British North West Company (a 20-year-old clerk who preferred to write in English) kept a journal of his visits to Fort Mandan, where the captains warned him not to hand out British flags and medals. Far to the south, Spanish officials read the expedition as an invasion. The Commandant General of the Interior Provinces, Nemesio Salcedo, ordered New Mexico to send armed parties to find and stop the Americans. They never did. Salcedo's record is administrative: dated orders rather than a diary, and it is tagged that way.
.intro[4] = This chapter has seven clusters where several eyes describe the same day: the departure on 14 May 1804; Floyd's death on 20 August 1804; the standoff with the Teton Lakota on 25 September 1804; the meetings with Larocque at Fort Mandan in late November 1804; Camp Fortunate on 17 August 1805, when Sacagawea recognized her own people; Clark's 'Ocian in view!' on 7 November 1805, written on the Columbia estuary about 20 miles short of the sea; and the return to St. Louis on 23 September 1806. Quotations keep the writers' own spelling. The journals' language about Native peoples is of its time. Nations are named here by their own names, with the expedition's names in brackets.
.calendar_note = All dates are Gregorian, as the journals kept them. Many entries were written up or copied later: Clark kept field notes and then rewrote them in notebooks, Gass's text was revised for print in 1807, and Whitehouse's journal also survives in a later paraphrase. The Dakota winter counts do not use days. Each image names a whole winter-year (roughly first snow to first snow), so those entries carry precision 'range' and are pinned to 1 December only so that they sort in the right place.
