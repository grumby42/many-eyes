## vantage_key
0 = a principal actor or those directly under attack (the President, Franco, the Alcázar garrison, the air commander at Guernica); 1 = eyewitness in the line or the street; 2 = in the same besieged city; 3 = in the same country or at its border, at a distance; 4 = abroad, deciding or hearing from a foreign capital.
## intro:0
On 17 and 18 July 1936 army garrisons in Spanish Morocco and then across the peninsula rose against the government of the Second Republic. In some cities the rising won within hours; in others, including Madrid and Barcelona, it was put down by loyal police and by workers' militias. What was meant to be a quick coup became a war of nearly three years, fought between the Republic and the generals who soon named Francisco Franco their leader, and drawn into the wider struggle of the 1930s by German and Italian help for one side and Soviet help and foreign volunteers for the other.
## intro:1
This chapter sets side by side people who wrote on dated pages as it happened, from both camps and from outside. On the Republican side are the President of the Republic, Manuel Azaña, in his private notebooks; the Soviet journalist Mikhail Koltsov; and three foreign volunteers, John Cornford, George Orwell and Alvah Bessie. On the Nationalist side are Franco's own dated proclamations, the besieged garrison of the Alcázar of Toledo and its commander, the Primate of Spain, Cardinal Isidro Gomá, the German air commander Wolfram von Richthofen, and Italy's foreign minister, Galeazzo Ciano. Between and around them stand three observers: the United States ambassador, Claude Bowers; the Chilean diplomat Carlos Morla Lynch, who sheltered people from both sides in his embassy in Madrid; and George Steer of The Times.
## intro:2
The honest record here is mixed, and the forms are labelled. Some sources are true diaries (Azaña, Koltsov, Richthofen, Bessie, Ciano, Morla Lynch). Others are dated letters (Cornford), diplomatic telegrams (Bowers), newspaper dispatches (Steer), official proclamations and communiqués (Franco), a siege bulletin (the Alcázar), church documents (Gomá) and one memoir written a few months afterwards (Orwell's Homage to Catalonia). Some diaries were reworked before they were published: Koltsov's was edited for Soviet readers in 1938 and gives some of his own doings to a figure called 'Miguel Martínez'; Richthofen's April 1937 pages were written up from shorthand notes in May.
## intro:3
These are twentieth-century texts still under copyright, so every entry is a summary in our own words. The only quotations are three world-famous phrases. Where the two sides told the same event in different ways, as with the bombing of Guernica, the defenders of the Alcázar, or the May fighting in Barcelona, the chapter says what each source recorded and leaves the judging to the reader.
## intro:4
Five clusters hold the chapter together: the rising and the first resistance in July 1936; the defence of Madrid in November 1936; Guernica on 26–30 April 1937; the fighting in Barcelona in May 1937 and the change of government that followed; and January–April 1939, when Barcelona fell, the President crossed into France, Madrid was occupied and the war was declared over.
